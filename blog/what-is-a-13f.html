What is SEC Form 13F—and what can it tell you?

Published: October 24, 2026 · Updated: October 25, 2026 · By IO Innovation

Form 13F gives the public a delayed look at certain securities reported by large institutional investment managers. Learn who files, what appears in a report, and how to use the data without mistaking a quarter-end snapshot for a live portfolio or a trade recommendation.

The key idea: Form 13F reports certain holdings as of a past quarter-end. It generally does not show what a manager owns now, the exact date a position changed, or why an investment was made.

In general, an institutional investment manager exercising investment discretion over at least $100 million in Section 13(f) securities may have a reporting obligation. The rule has specific measurement and filing requirements, so this is not a simple test of a firm’s total assets under management. Check current SEC guidance for the details that apply to a particular manager or filing period.

What is reported on Form 13F?

Form 13F is filed under Section 13(f) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. Its information table lists securities that are reportable under the SEC’s rules and the official Section 13(f) securities list. Entries generally include an issuer and security class, the number of shares or principal amount, and a reported value for the quarter-end position.

The report is a partial view—not a complete account of an institution’s investments or balance sheet. Not every asset is reportable. Cash, many private investments, and securities outside the applicable list may not appear. Short positions are not reported as a simple offset against long positions, and options or other reportable instruments need to be interpreted from their specific filing fields rather than assumed to have a particular economic meaning.

Do not equate reported value with today’s value. A filing records information for its reporting date. Prices, share counts, and portfolio composition may have changed since then.

Who files a 13F?

The rules cover certain institutional investment managers that exercise investment discretion over reportable securities and meet the applicable threshold. Depending on their activities, managers can include investment advisers, banks, insurance companies, broker-dealers, pension managers, and other institutions. The filing obligation is based on the relevant securities and regulatory rules—not simply on whether an organization calls itself a hedge fund or has a particular amount of total assets.

A large personal investor is not automatically a Form 13F filer just because their portfolio exceeds $100 million; the requirements concern institutional investment managers and their investment discretion. For eligibility questions, use the SEC’s current Form 13F FAQs or consult qualified legal counsel.

When are 13F reports filed?

Reports are generally due no later than 45 days after the end of each calendar quarter. That gap is central to interpreting the data: the filing becomes public after the date represented by the holdings.

  1. 01
    Quarter-end

    The snapshot date is the last day of the calendar quarter.

  2. 02
    Filing window

    The manager submits the report within the applicable deadline, generally 45 days after quarter-end.

  3. 03
    Public disclosure

    Researchers can review the report after it is filed, but the manager may have changed positions in the meantime.

Weekends and federal holidays can affect a specific due date. Verify the applicable deadline and filing record in SEC EDGAR rather than relying on a calendar estimate.

A reported value can change because the share count changed, the security’s market price changed, or both. A quarter-to-quarter comparison is not a transaction ledger. Check reported share amounts, security types, amendments, and the original source filing before drawing conclusions.

Why investors look at 13F filings

  • Research reported ownership. A filing can show whether a manager reported a particular eligible security at quarter-end.
  • Generate follow-up questions. Comparing periods can identify positions that appeared, disappeared, or changed in reported size.
  • Compare disclosures. Side-by-side reports can help organize research into holdings reported by different managers.

These are research clues, not conclusions about investment skill, current conviction, or future performance. Filings do not explain the manager’s rationale and do not account for every exposure or hedge.

What a 13F cannot tell you

  • What the manager owns today. The report reflects a past quarter-end and can be weeks old when published.
  • Exactly when or at what price a trade happened. Quarter-to-quarter changes do not show a complete transaction history.
  • The whole portfolio or its net exposure. The reporting rules exclude many assets and do not provide a full balance sheet.
  • The manager’s reasoning. A filing does not include the investment thesis, risk limits, or full hedging strategy.
  • A recommendation to copy a position. Another investor’s goals, time horizon, risk tolerance, liquidity needs, and tax situation can be very different.
Common mistake: treating a newly disclosed holding as a current buy signal. By publication, the position or market price may have changed materially.

A practical checklist for reading a filing

  1. Check both the report period and the filing date; they describe different points in time.
  2. Confirm the filer identity and reporting manager in the original SEC filing.
  3. Read issuer, class, CUSIP, share or principal amount, value, and option fields together.
  4. Look for amendments and other filing notes before comparing reports.
  5. When a number matters, open the original SEC document and verify the underlying information table.
  6. Use the data alongside company filings, valuation, risks, and your own research—not as a substitute for them.
