How to read Form 4: transaction codes and context

Published: August 18, 2026 · Updated: October 20, 2026 · By IO Innovation

Form 4 reports many transactions in a company’s securities by directors, officers, and certain large shareholders. The letter codes are a starting point, not a verdict: read them alongside the transaction details, footnotes, ownership after the transaction, and the insider’s role.

What Form 4 tells you

In most cases, a reportable transaction must be reported within two business days after it occurs, though exceptions and special rules apply. The filing identifies the reporting person and issuer and provides transaction dates, security type, amount, price where applicable, and holdings after the transaction. Form 4 can cover both ordinary shares and derivative securities such as options.

Do not confuse the transaction date with the filing date. A filing is a disclosure submitted after an event; it is not a live notification that an insider is trading at that moment.

Common transaction codes

Codes describe the reported transaction type. They do not, by themselves, establish whether the event reflects a discretionary investment decision.

Code Common description What to check
P Purchase on an exchange or from another person Review price, amount, ownership after, and footnotes.
S Sale on an exchange or to another person A sale alone does not reveal motive; consider scale and remaining stake.
A Grant, award, or other acquisition from the company Often connected to compensation; check vesting and footnotes.
M Exercise or conversion of a derivative security Separate the exercise from any related sale reported in another row.
F Securities withheld or used to pay an exercise price or tax liability Not equivalent to an ordinary open-market sale.
G Gift of securities to or from the reporting person A gift is not an exchange purchase or sale.
D Disposition or transfer back to the issuer Read the footnote for the transaction’s circumstances.

This is a selection of commonly encountered codes, not a complete codebook. SEC form instructions and filing footnotes control when interpreting a particular report.

Read the details around the code

  1. Identify the person and role. An officer, director, and 10% owner may have different relationships to the company; check the reporting-owner section.
  2. Check direct versus indirect ownership. Securities can be held through a trust, partnership, or other entity. Read the ownership code and explanatory footnotes rather than assuming the insider personally holds every share.
  3. Separate transaction rows. An option exercise and a later sale can appear as distinct transactions. Summarize each event before characterizing the net change.
  4. Look at holdings after the event. A sale can be a small fraction of the person’s remaining stake—or a large reduction. Use comparable share counts and account for derivatives and indirect holdings.
  5. Read the footnotes and plan disclosures. Explanations may describe tax withholding, vesting, gifts, or a Rule 10b5-1 trading plan. A plan-related checkbox is context, not proof of motive or a guarantee about execution conditions.

Common misreadings to avoid

  • “An insider sold, so they must expect a decline.” Insiders may sell for liquidity, diversification, taxes, or other reasons. The filing alone usually cannot tell you why.
  • “A Form 4 row means open-market trading.” Awards, option exercises, tax withholding, gifts, and issuer transactions can all be reported.
  • “The headline dollar value is the whole story.” Price, share count, derivative status, and post-transaction holdings all matter.
  • “One person’s transaction represents the whole company.” Look for independently reported activity by multiple insiders and review each person’s role and ownership.

A quick reading checklist

  1. Confirm the issuer, reporting person, role, and filing date.
  2. Find the transaction date, security, code, amount, and price.
  3. Check direct or indirect ownership and holdings after the transaction.
  4. Read footnotes, derivative rows, and any trading-plan disclosure.
  5. Compare with earlier filings for the same person, then research the company on its own merits.

Form 3 generally reports initial ownership when someone becomes subject to insider reporting; Form 5 is used for certain transactions that were not reported earlier when permitted or required. Neither changes the need to check the underlying filing and its instructions.
