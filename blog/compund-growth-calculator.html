Compound growth: how time and contributions shape a projection

Published: October 24, 2026 · By IO Innovation · 7 minute read

Compounding means returns can earn returns of their own. Add regular contributions and more time, and a hypothetical balance can grow substantially—but the result depends on assumptions, and real investment returns are neither smooth nor guaranteed.

The basics A simple example Try the calculator Understand the assumptions

What compound growth means

With simple growth, each period’s return is calculated only on the original amount. With compounding, prior gains remain in the balance, so future returns are calculated on the original amount and accumulated gains. Regular deposits add another source of growth: each contribution has its own time in the account.

A simplified lump-sum formula is future value = starting balance × (1 + periodic return)number of periods. A real projection with ongoing deposits adds each contribution at the selected time and then applies the assumed growth rate.

Keep the distinction clear: a projected ending balance includes both the money contributed and the hypothetical growth. Those are different parts of the result.

A simple illustration: time changes the math

Suppose $5,000 were left to grow for 30 years with no additional deposits. At a constant assumed annual rate, the arithmetic would look like this:

Assumed annual rate Approximate value after 30 years Approximate growth
3% $12,136 $7,136
5% $21,610 $16,610
7% $38,061 $33,061

Rounded mathematical examples using annual compounding on a one-time $5,000 deposit. They exclude taxes, fees, inflation, deposits, and withdrawals; they are not forecasts or expected returns.

The gap widens because each year’s growth builds on the previous balance. This is why changing the assumed rate can have a large effect over long periods—and why a high assumption should never be mistaken for a likely or suitable outcome.

Important assumptions

This educational calculator is not investment, tax, or financial advice. Results are hypothetical and not guaranteed. It assumes a constant annual return converted to a monthly rate, regular contributions at the selected timing, and no taxes, fees, or contribution changes. Actual returns vary and may be negative. Inflation-adjusted values are approximations, and currency selection only changes the display format.

How to choose and interpret the inputs

Starting balance and contributions

Use amounts that fit the scenario you want to examine. The calculator adds contributions on the selected monthly, quarterly, or annual schedule. Deposits at the beginning of a period receive more time to grow than deposits at the end.

Assumed annual return

Treat this as a sensitivity input, not a promise or a forecast. Run several lower and higher assumptions and consider that actual returns fluctuate rather than arriving at the same rate each year.

Time horizon

Choose a period that matches the question you are exploring. The longer the period, the more the result depends on the assumed rate and the more uncertain a fixed-rate projection becomes.

Inflation and currency

The inflation-adjusted amount estimates future purchasing power in today’s terms. The currency selector changes formatting only; it does not convert the balances or account for differences in local inflation.

Compare scenarios fairly: change one assumption at a time, record the inputs, and compare total invested separately from estimated growth. A calculator cannot determine whether a savings or investment choice is right for your circumstances.

What a calculator leaves out

  • Uneven returns: markets do not earn a steady return every month or year, and losses are possible.
  • Fees and taxes: costs and tax treatment vary and can reduce what remains. They are not included in this projection.
  • Changing contributions: the calculator assumes the selected contribution continues unchanged for the full period.
  • Personal circumstances: an arithmetic projection cannot assess your goals, risk tolerance, cash needs, or suitability.

Use the result to understand how assumptions affect the arithmetic—not as a target balance or a reason to take more risk. Consider consulting a qualified professional for advice about your situation.
